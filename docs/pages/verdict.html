What works and what fails

Works Run it at production scale

  • Run MAIN and PR on a zero-copy clone of production data, or run only the PR and compare it with the model's own production history.
  • Replay the capacity pool's billing over a month.
  • In the lab the full-clone A/B got the direction of every material change right and caught every large regression. Its median magnitude error was about 10%, and 96% of its estimates landed within one magnitude bucket.
  • The PR-only variant costs about half as much: 94% direction accuracy, one miss.

Fails Estimate without running

  • Static query plans.
  • Dry-run-style byte counts.
  • Four ways of sampling the data.
  • Extrapolating from two sample sizes.
  • Each made wrong-sign or missed-material errors on realistic PRs. Learning a correction from past PRs made the sample-based methods worse.

Consequences

  • CI cost. A reliable check costs 2.5–4.6 production runs of the affected workloads per analysis. It pays off only for models that run more than about 40–70 times a month.
  • CI conditions. CI has to run on the production warehouse size. A smaller CI engine overstated changes by up to 136% and flipped one sign.
  • Dollars. The dollar figure is unambiguous only on dedicated or per-query-billed compute. On shared or prepaid capacity, the marginal dollar depends on a pricing policy and can have the opposite sign to the attributed dollar.

Question 1

Can this be built?

Yes, as five pieces. All five were built and exercised end to end in the lab.

  • Change detection: dbt state:modified plus a diff of the compiled SQL.
  • Workload mapping that includes consumers: dbt lineage plus the platform's access history.
  • A measurement policy: skip workloads whose plans are identical, run the changed ones at production scale, and repeat cheap statements until the timing is stable.
  • A monthly replay of the pool's billing with an explicit pricing policy.
  • Graded intervals instead of point estimates.

A model that predicts production cost from code, plans, bytes or samples was not decision-grade on this benchmark.

What accuracy is realistic?

The full clone's false warnings all sat near the ±10% materiality threshold. On a real platform expect worse, because in the lab the clone was the production data. When the lab introduced real-world gaps deliberately:

  • a smaller CI engine overstated ratios by up to 136% and flipped one sign;
  • concurrent production load shifted ratios by −18% to +58%;
  • cold single runs understated ratios by up to 29%.

A realistic target is the right direction and a magnitude within one bucket for high-grade cases, reported as dollar ranges. The brief's 90% bars are plausible only for that class, measured at production scale.

Question 4

What cannot be predicted before deployment?

  • Frequency changes made outside the repository, and seasonality. Unseen seasonal peaks were missed by 67%; sparse ad-hoc jobs were off by about 100%.
  • The sign and size of scale-dependent changes when CI data is smaller than production. One change was 27% cheaper at production scale and a regression on samples. A 14× regression looked like +4% in a 1% sample.
  • Marginal dollars on shared or prepaid capacity until someone chooses a pricing policy. Telemetry cannot supply that policy.
  • Scale-out steps and contention caused by concurrent load, beyond an interval.
  • Serverless side-costs such as auto-clustering and materialised-view refresh, where the vendors' own estimators are off by 50–100%.
  • Python and Spark jobs on shared all-purpose clusters.
  • Negotiated Databricks prices, which the platform does not expose.
  • Costs that only production conditions produce: optimiser learning, cache state, and effects between workloads.
Question 5

Can uncertainty be represented honestly and usefully?

For measurement noise, yes. 80% intervals built from measured run-to-run variance covered the true value 84% of the time for the full clone and 80% for the hybrid. The grade derived from each interval predicted correctness: high-grade calls were right on direction every time, medium-grade calls 67% of the time.

Honest reporting also needs three things:

  • wider intervals for CI-versus-production gaps, frequency, and pricing policy;
  • a label saying whether the dollar figure is marginal or attributed;
  • a "not measurable" result for low and not-viable classes.

A point estimate should never be shown on its own.

Question 6

Does a common abstraction survive across Snowflake, Databricks and BigQuery?

Yes, with a different object from the one proposed. What survives is a usage change per workload, replayed through each capacity pool's billing function:

CostImpact = Σ over pools [ Bill(month with PR usage) − Bill(month with MAIN usage) ]

One engine can run this, with a thin adapter per platform for measurement, telemetry and billing. "Cost per query × frequency" fails as the abstraction. In the simulation, the same measured change cost +$208 a month on a Snowflake warehouse and +$1 under BigQuery on-demand. A change to dashboard queries showed +$57 attributed and −$11 marginal on a busy warehouse.

  • Snowflake (dedicated warehouses) is the cleanest fit.
  • BigQuery on-demand is easy and already commodity.
  • Databricks is the weakest for a CI verdict: billing arrives up to 24 hours late, the disk cache cannot be disabled, VM cost sits outside the platform, and negotiated prices are invisible.

Question 8

What is the narrowest credible V1?

A cost-regression gate for frequently run dbt models on dedicated Snowflake warehouses.

  • Scope. Models that run at least daily, with hourly models as the sweet spot.
  • Detection. state:modified combined with a compiled-SQL diff.
  • What runs. Changed models, their direct children, and the queries that read changed tables (from ACCESS_HISTORY). Deeper descendants are screened and skipped when their plan and row counts are unchanged.
  • Measurement. One PR-only run on a zero-copy clone, compared with production history, on a single-cluster CI warehouse of the production size, with the result cache off. Use an A/B run when history is thin or noisy. Repeat millisecond statements until stable.
  • Economics. Replay the dedicated warehouse's billing. For dashboard queries on shared warehouses, report the usage change and an attributed dollar range, never a marginal figure.
  • Output. Warn only on a rise of at least 50%, or above a dollar threshold, at high grade, and list the drivers. Everything else is informational. A per-PR compute cap applies, with an explicit "too expensive to measure" result.
  • Out of scope. Databricks, BigQuery editions, Python models, serverless features, sparse or seasonal jobs, and any claim of predicting cost without running the change.

Question 9

What evidence is still missing?

  • Any live-platform measurement. Does a CI run predict the production warehouse's ratio on Snowflake? Is Snowflake's run-to-run noise like the lab's? Does Snowflake's per-query attribution track execution time? Does the billing replay reproduce the metered bill?
  • A real PR corpus. The base rate of material regressions, their frequency mix, and how many changes a static screen could clear. The lab's 26 PRs were written by hand and carry no base rates.
  • Real production telemetry. Schedules, consumers and shared-pool load decide how much spend sits in the high-grade class at all.
  • A check of the billing replay against metered bills.
  • Tolerance. Will teams accept 2.5–4.6 production runs of CI compute, and the added wait, on every PR that touches their busiest models?
Question 10

What is the next rational step?

Narrow the concept and run one more technical experiment before building an MVP. Run customer discovery in parallel.

  • Technical, 1–2 days, at most 60 Snowflake credits. Run the prepared live Snowflake experiment. Then replay a real dbt repository's last 20–50 merged PRs and check whether the V1 gate would have flagged the changes that raised the bill.
  • Discovery. Test the narrow offer: would a team pay three to five production runs of compute per PR on its busiest models to block large cost regressions, and does it want marginal or attributed dollars on shared warehouses?
  • Not now. The multi-platform product, the combined Snowflake, Databricks, Anthropic and Datadog comment, and an ML cost predictor. The evidence does not support them.

Go Build an MVP if both hold

  • High-grade verdicts keep at least 90% direction accuracy on the live platform.
  • The replayed PRs show material regressions in frequently run models often enough to justify the CI spend.

Stop Stop if either holds

  • Live CI-to-production transfer errors push high-grade direction accuracy below about 90%.
  • Material regressions in frequently run models turn out to be rare. What remains then is dbt's cost insights plus alerting, which already exists.
